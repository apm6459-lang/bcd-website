Chapter 7 Bankruptcy

Understanding the Chapter 7 Process

Chapter 7 is a type of bankruptcy commonly referred to as liquidation. Unlike Chapter 13, it does not involve creating a repayment plan.

A bankruptcy trustee may gather and sell property that is not protected by applicable exemptions and use the proceeds to pay creditors. Many individual Chapter 7 cases, however, are no-asset cases in which there is no distribution to unsecured creditors.

For individuals who qualify, Chapter 7 may result in the discharge of certain debts and provide an opportunity for a financial fresh start.

What Is Chapter 7 Bankruptcy?

Chapter 7 is a form of bankruptcy that does not involve creating a repayment plan like Chapter 13. A bankruptcy trustee is appointed to administer the case.

For individuals who qualify, Chapter 7 may result in the discharge of certain debts. Some debts, however, may not be dischargeable.

How Does Chapter 7 Work?

After a Chapter 7 case is filed, a trustee is appointed. The trustee reviews the bankruptcy case and may gather and sell property that is not protected by applicable exemptions.

U.S. Courts explains that many Chapter 7 cases involving individuals are no-asset cases in which there is no distribution to unsecured creditors.

How Long Does Chapter 7 Take?

The exact timeline depends on the case. For many individual Chapter 7 cases, the process from filing to discharge may take several months.

A discharge generally cannot be entered until applicable deadlines and requirements have been satisfied.

What Happens After I File Chapter 7?

After the petition is filed, the bankruptcy case is opened and a trustee is assigned. Creditors generally receive notice of the bankruptcy, and important dates and deadlines are established.

Filing also generally puts the automatic stay into effect. The automatic stay stops many collection actions while the bankruptcy case is pending, although exceptions and limitations can apply.

What Happens at the Chapter 7 341 Meeting?

The meeting of creditors, commonly called the 341 meeting, is conducted by the bankruptcy trustee rather than a judge.

The debtor answers questions under oath about the bankruptcy case. Creditors may attend and ask questions, although they are not required to attend.

Learn more on BCD's 341 Meeting resource page .

When Does a Chapter 7 Discharge Happen?

If the debtor satisfies the applicable requirements and no objection or other issue prevents discharge, the bankruptcy court may enter a discharge later in the Chapter 7 case.

A discharge releases the debtor from personal liability for certain debts and generally prevents creditors from trying to collect debts that were discharged.

Learn more on BCD's Discharge resource page .
